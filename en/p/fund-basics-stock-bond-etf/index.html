Investment Fund Basics: Stocks, Bonds, ETFs, and Mutual Funds

When you buy a fund, what you are really buying is its underlying assets. A fund is only as good as what it holds.

1. The Three Foundational Assets: Stocks, Bonds, and Funds

1.1 What Is a Stock

A stock = a fractional ownership stake in a company.

Once you own shares, there are two ways to make money:

Method Description
Dividends The company distributes part of its profits to shareholders
Capital gains Selling your shares to someone else at a higher price

Why would someone pay more for your shares? Because they expect the company to earn more and pay out more in the future. By selling, you are effectively cashing out that future expectation today.

A stock has never been a lottery ticket. Its price is the market’s collective judgment of the company’s future earning power. What you buy is a piece of ownership—not a number bouncing around on a screen.

1.2 What Is a Bond

A bond = a loan contract. You lend money to an issuer, who pays you interest on a regular schedule and returns the principal plus the final interest payment at maturity.

Bond Type Issuer Risk Level
Government bonds Sovereign state Virtually risk-free (principal and interest are almost certain)
Corporate bonds Companies Carry risk (the issuer may default)

Key concept: interest rates and bond prices move in opposite directions

  • When market rates fall → existing bonds with higher fixed coupons become more attractive → bond prices rise (good for bondholders)
  • When market rates rise → existing bonds with lower fixed coupons become less attractive → bond prices fall (bad for bondholders)

Duration: the weighted-average time it takes for a bond investor to receive all cash flows. The longer the duration, the more sensitive the bond price is to interest rate changes.

  • If you expect rates to fall → extend duration (favour long-term bonds for greater price gains)
  • If you expect rates to rise → shorten duration (favour short-term bonds to limit losses)

Yield to maturity vs. coupon rate: If you buy a bond with a face value of $100 and a 3% coupon for only $80, your yield to maturity is far higher than 3%—because you bought at a discount and will pocket both the coupon payments and the difference between the purchase price and face value at maturity.

1.3 What Is a Fund

A fund = pooled money, managed by a professional, invested collectively into stocks, bonds, gold, and other assets.

  • Actively managed funds: The manager actively times the market, picks stocks, and adjusts the portfolio. Performance hinges entirely on the manager’s skill.
  • Passive funds (index funds): The fund tracks a pre-defined portfolio (an index) and makes no subjective stock-picking decisions.

Key takeaway: The vast majority of actively managed funds underperform passive index funds over the long run. This is not an opinion—it is a statistical fact, and one that Warren Buffett validated with his famous ten-year, million-dollar bet.

2. A-Share Market Basics

2.1 What You Can Trade

Market Eligibility Daily Price Limit
A-share Main Board No threshold ±10%
STAR Market (SSE STAR) ¥500K in assets ±20%
Hong Kong stocks (H-shares) ¥500K in assets (via Stock Connect) No limit

How to trade: Brokerages act as intermediaries between investors and the exchange. You must open an account with a brokerage to participate.

2.2 Trading Costs (Friction)

Every buy or sell order incurs the following fees:

Fee When buying When selling
Stamp duty Not charged Charged (seller only)
Transfer fee Charged to both sides Charged to both sides
Brokerage commission Charged (¥5 minimum) Charged (¥5 minimum)

⚠️ Heads-up: the ¥5 minimum commission can significantly impact small trades. Avoid frequent trading—frictional costs will quietly eat away your returns.

2.3 A-Share Taxation

Tax Type Rule
Capital gains tax Not levied (profits from trading are not taxed)
Dividend tax Withheld upon dividend distribution; the longer your holding period, the lower the rate
Stamp duty Charged on the seller only, at the time of sale

2.4 Key Terms Cheat Sheet

Term Meaning
Open a position (开仓) Buy an asset to establish a holding
Hold a position (持仓) The assets you currently own
Close a position (平仓) Sell an asset to exit a holding
Position size (仓位) The proportion of total capital allocated to a given asset
Going long (做多) Buy an asset hoping to sell it later at a higher price
Short selling (做空) Borrow shares, sell them, then buy back at a lower price (requires ¥500K threshold)
One lot (一手) 100 shares—the minimum trading unit on the A-share market
Candlestick / K-line (K 线) A chart showing the opening, closing, high, and low prices for a given period

3. ETFs (Exchange-Traded Funds) vs. Mutual Funds

3.1 Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs)

ETF = Exchange Traded Fund.

  • How to buy: Exactly like buying a stock—real-time trading through your brokerage app
  • Price: Determined by live matching of buy and sell orders on the exchange; may deviate from the fund’s net asset value (creating a premium or discount)
  • Fees: Extremely low. For example, a broad Shanghai Composite ETF can have a total expense ratio as low as 0.2% per year
  • Why so cheap: ETFs passively track an index—no manager actively trading, no need to manage cash flows from retail investors entering and exiting

Premium/Discount rate = (ETF market price − NAV) / NAV. A positive value means the ETF trades above its true value (premium); a negative value means it trades below (discount).

3.2 Mutual Funds (Off-Exchange Funds)

  • How to buy: Subscribe or redeem directly through platforms like Alipay or Tiantian Fund—not through exchange order matching
  • Confirmation time: If you subscribe on day T, your shares are confirmed on T+1
  • Fees: Generally higher than comparable ETFs
  • Why more expensive: Fund companies must actively manage cash flows from frequent retail subscriptions and redemptions, incurring higher operational costs

3.3 Side-by-Side Comparison

ETF (On-Exchange) Mutual Fund (Off-Exchange)
Trading method Real-time, like a stock Subscribe / redeem with the fund company
Speed Instant execution T+1 confirmation
Expense ratio Very low (~0.2% p.a.) Higher
Premium / Discount Possible Not applicable (traded at NAV)
Barrier to entry Requires a securities brokerage account Alipay / fund apps work fine

💡 Rule of thumb: Go with ETFs if you want the lowest fees and plan to hold long-term. Choose mutual funds if you prioritise convenience and small regular investments. Over decades, fee differences compound into significant return gaps.

4. Reverse Repo with Government Bonds

A short-term cash-management tool with risk anchored to short-dated government bonds and near-zero credit risk.

How it works:

  1. You lend your cash to the exchange
  2. The borrower pledges government bonds as collateral to the exchange
  3. At maturity, the exchange returns the bonds to the borrower, and you collect your principal plus interest
  4. If the borrower cannot repay, the exchange can liquidate the pledged bonds to cover the obligation

Safety: With government bonds as collateral, your principal is virtually risk-free. An ideal parking spot for idle cash.

Annualised reference return: roughly 1%–1.5% (slightly better than money-market funds like Yu’e Bao).

5. Investing vs. Speculating: The Fundamental Distinction

Benjamin Graham’s definition of investment: An investment operation is one which, upon thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and an adequate return. Operations not meeting these requirements are speculative.

The Investor’s Checklist

  • Does the company make money? How much? From what source?
  • How long can its profit growth be sustained?
  • What does the competitive landscape look like?
  • Is the current valuation reasonable?

The Speculator’s Playbook

  • Buying on a hunch—“I just feel like it’s going up”
  • Staring at candlestick charts, trying to divine answers from patterns
  • Chasing hot themes, swing-trading, timing the market
  • Frequent switching between tickers in pursuit of short-term spreads

A candlestick chart tells you exactly two things and nothing more:

  1. At what price the market currently values this company
  2. At what price the market has valued it in the past

That is all. Past prices do not influence future prices.

A Story Worth Reflecting On

Look at NVIDIA’s candlestick chart from 2020: a noticeable drawdown, a slow recovery, then a relentless march to new all-time highs. If all you saw was the chart, tripling your money might have felt like a clear signal to sell. But if you understood the company’s future growth story, you would not have sold—and NVIDIA went on to multiply many more times from that point.

If your only reason for selling is “it looks like it has gone up too much, it feels risky,” you may well miss a once-in-a-lifetime wealth-building opportunity.

6. What Risk Really Means

Academia defines risk as volatility (variance). But true risk is not how much a price fluctuates—it is the risk of permanent capital loss.

  • If a company’s competitive moat disappears, its earning power is destroyed, and its business model enters irreversible decline—no matter how calm the stock price looks, the risk is extreme
  • If a company has durable earning power and steadily growing free cash flow—even if the stock price whipsaws violently along the way, as long as you do not sell at the wrong moment, you will not suffer permanent capital loss

How to Weather Volatility

Strategy Description
Don’t go all-in Never bet your entire net worth on a single company—that is gambling
Position sizing Keep only ~10% of your portfolio in highly volatile names; the other 90% stays diversified
Keep powder dry Maintain a cash reserve so you have room to average down and correct mistakes

Your willingness to endure periods of zero return—or even paper losses—determines the level of return you can realistically aim for.

The road of investing is long. Get the fundamentals right first, and you will not be led around by market sentiment.
