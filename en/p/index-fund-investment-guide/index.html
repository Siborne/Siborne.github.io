A Practical Guide to Index Fund Dollar-Cost Averaging: From Valuation to Take-Profit

A complete practical guide to index fund dollar-cost averaging (DCA), covering why index funds, the smile curve principle, PE-based market timing, three major broad-market indices, DCA strategies for salaried workers, the pyramid accumulation method, and the 3331 take-profit strategy.

1. Why Index Funds?

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) is the best friend an ordinary person will ever have. The 10,000-hour rule does not apply in investing — choices matter 10,000 times more than effort.

1.1 Indices Are Immortal; Companies Die

Stocks come and go, but indices persist. Of the original S&P 500 constituents from 1957, only about 10% remain today — the rest were acquired or removed. Of the first 100 stocks on China’s ChiNext board, only 16 survive.

Indices have a strict self-cleansing mechanism: weak companies get kicked out, strong ones stay in. The index constantly renews itself.

An index is immortal; individual stocks are mortal.

1.2 Long-Term Annualized Returns Are Solid

Index Annualized Return (Including Reinvested Dividends)
S&P 500 ~10% (since inception)
CSI 300 ~9.2% (since 2004, with dividend reinvestment)
CSI 1000 ~11% (with dividend reinvestment)
ChiNext Index ~8.8%

1.3 Buffett’s 10-Year Bet

In 2007, a Wall Street fund manager challenged Warren Buffett to a 10-year, million-dollar bet: five top actively managed funds versus the S&P 500 index fund.

10 years later (2017), the results were in:

  • The five competing funds delivered annualized returns from 0.3% to 6.5% at best
  • The S&P 500 index fund returned 8.5% annualized
  • Every single active fund lost.

Buffett’s conclusion:

“By periodically investing in an index fund, the know-nothing investor can actually outperform most professional fund managers.”

1.4 Why Individual Stocks Are a Losing Game

Buying individual stocks means owning a slice of a single company and bearing extreme concentration risk. You need to answer: Is the company profitable? What is the P/E ratio? Is the business model sound? Are the financials reliable? What about management quality? Will the business become obsolete?

In the stock market, the real distribution is roughly 70% losers, 20% breaking even, and 10% winning. Most people who make money got lucky. Most people who lose money — that was inevitable.

2. Why Does DCA Work?

2.1 The Smile Curve

The essence of DCA: automatically buying more shares during market declines, thereby lowering your overall cost basis.

Consider this extreme real-world case:

On June 18, 2015, the CSI 300 stood near its all-time high. An investor began investing 2,000 yuan per month into a CSI 300 index fund — at the absolute peak of a bubble. By November 30, 2016, the index had crashed to 3,412 points. After 18 months of buying into a falling market, the total return was +8.58%.

How can you make money during a crash?

  • Early purchases were indeed underwater
  • But as prices dropped, the same 2,000 yuan bought more shares each month
  • The overall cost basis was dragged way down
  • It only took a modest recovery to turn profitable

2.2 Disciplined Contrarianism

When everyone else is panic-selling, you are DCA-ing. You know the index cannot die. You buy coldly, methodically. You might even start feeling good about getting cheaper shares.

DCA profits are, at their core, a reward for going against human nature.

2.3 DCA vs. Lump Sum

Method Advantages Disadvantages
DCA No market timing needed, smooths cost basis, disciplined Underperforms lump sum in a one-way bull market
Lump Sum Maximizes returns in a bull run Can mean years of losses if bought at the top

3. When to Start? — The Art of Valuation

3.1 The Key Metric: P/E Ratio

P/E (Price-to-Earnings) answers: how many years of earnings will it take to recoup your investment?

For an index, we look at the P/E historical percentile:

P/E Percentile Meaning Action
Below 40% Cheap Begin DCA
40%–80% Fair Hold, do not sell
Above 80% Expensive Prepare to sell

3.2 Tools for Checking Valuations

Tool How to Use
Qieman App Home → Market Temperature → Index Temperature
Xueqiu Fund App Home → Index Valuation → Check P/E percentile
THS (Tonghuashun) Search “Index Valuation Tool”
Rational Investor (lixingren.com) Professional valuation data
Ask an AI directly “What are the current P/E historical percentiles for major indices?”

When checking valuations, always look at 3-year, 5-year, and 10-year data to avoid being misled by short-term noise.

3.3 Special Note on ChiNext

ChiNext is more volatile. Check both P/E and P/B (Price-to-Book) to form a valuation judgment. Use the more conservative of the two.

4. Which Index to Choose?

4.1 Comparing Three Major Broad-Market Indices

Index Characteristics Volatility Best For
CSI 300 Large-cap blue chips, stable Low Conservative investors
CSI 500 Mid-cap growth Medium Balanced investors
ChiNext Small-cap high-growth, volatile High Return-seeking investors

Higher volatility means better DCA outcomes — you buy cheaper during drawdowns and rise faster during rallies.

Historical backtest (DCA from 2010 → sold at 2015 peak):

Index Total Return
ChiNext +185%
CSI 500 +120%
CSI 300 +63%

4.2 Don’t Put All Your Eggs in One Basket

China’s A-share market experiences structural bull runs — sometimes large caps rally, sometimes small caps do. Style rotation is the norm. Diversify across all three indices, adjusting proportions to fit your risk tolerance.

4.3 The Ultimate Lazy Option: S&P 500

If you want to skip the take-profit strategy entirely, pick an index with a persistently rising platform — the S&P 500.

  • US markets tend toward long bulls and short bears, trending northeast over decades
  • Even buying at the annual high every year yields ~10.6% annualized over 30 years
  • Buying at the annual low every year yields ~11.7%
  • The gap is narrow because the index’s floor keeps rising every year

Important: When buying S&P 500 QDII funds, watch out for premium rates and expense ratios. During periods of tight foreign exchange quotas, premiums can reach 30%, and subscriptions may be restricted.

4.4 A-Shares vs. US Stocks at a Glance

A-Shares (e.g. CSI 300) US Stocks (S&P 500)
Market Style High volatility, clear cycles Long bulls, short bears, rising floor
Must Take Profit? Yes — sell at high valuations Can hold indefinitely
Buy at annual high 5.63% annualized 10.6% annualized
Buy at annual low 10.69% annualized 11.7% annualized

5. How to Buy — Concrete Execution Plans

5.1 The Salaried Worker’s Plan (Simplest)

After payday each month:

  1. Deduct living expenses and essential spending
  2. Invest 70% of the remainder
  3. Buy weekly or monthly (over the long term, the day of the week makes no difference)

For a salaried worker, DCA is almost comically simple: paycheck arrives → subtract expenses → buy with the rest → done.

5.2 The Idle Cash Plan: Pyramid DCA Method

If you have a lump sum you won’t need for 3–5 years.

Allocation:

Use Share
Treasury bonds / reverse repos (ballast) 10%
Cash reserve (dry powder) 25%
DCA capital 65%

The Pyramid DCA Method (using a 3-year / 36-month cycle):

  1. Divide your DCA capital into 72 units (double the number of months, leaving room for accelerated buying)
  2. P/E percentile below 40% → buy 1 unit per month (narrow end of the pyramid)
  3. P/E percentile below 20% → buy 2 units per month (accelerate)
  4. P/E percentile below 1% → go bigger (extreme undervaluation, wide base of the pyramid)

Accelerated buying during crashes (if the market plunges right after you start):

  • 20% drop from peak → accelerate 3 months worth of purchases
  • 40% drop → deploy all remaining planned units immediately
  • Then resume normal monthly DCA

The index is immortal. You have no reason to panic. In fact, a crash should genuinely make you happy — shares just went on sale.

5.3 The Lazy Plan (S&P 500 Only)

No valuation checks. No take-profits. The only things to monitor are premium rates and expense ratios.

6. How to Sell — The Take-Profit Strategy

Selling is the hardest part. If you DCA into A-shares and fail to sell at the top, 3–5 years of disciplined investing will yield only mediocre results.

6.1 The 3331 Take-Profit Method

Valuation Point Action
P/E percentile hits 80% Automatically sell 30% of holdings
P/E percentile hits 90% Automatically sell another 30%
Approaches all-time highs Sell another 30%
Breaks through all-time highs Keep the final 10% — have some fun with it

6.2 Drawdown Protection

If the market stalls after reaching 80% or 90% percentiles and starts rolling over:

  • When it falls back to the 90% level, liquidate everything
  • Alternatively, leave the final 10% as observation

6.3 Only Take Certain Profits

When it is time to sell, sell decisively. We only take certain profits. The last frothy phase is for the final 10% — play with it lightly.

6.4 After You Liquidate

  • Spend a portion — reward yourself
  • Park the rest in treasury bonds or reverse repos
  • Patiently wait for the next undervaluation opportunity

7. The Power of Compound Interest

7.1 The Difference Across Annualized Returns

Starting with 1 million yuan at age 30:

Annualized Return At Age 60 (30 years) At Age 70 (40 years)
8% 10.06 million 21.72 million
10% 17.45 million 45.25 million
11% 22.89 million 65.00 million
15% 66.21 million 268 million

7.2 A 1% Gap Is Massive

A mere 1% difference in annualized return compounds into tens of millions over 40 years.

Beware of fees: over the long run, the gap between a 1.5% management fee and a 0.2% fee can eat up seven-figure sums. Always choose low-cost index funds.

7.3 Buffett’s Compounding Secret

Buffett’s formula is deceptively simple:

Longevity + high annualized returns = compounding magic.

The longer you live, the more ferocious the compounding in the later years. Start as early as possible — compound your knowledge and your money simultaneously.

8. What If Your DCA Is in the Red?

Losing money right after you start is completely normal. Here is the three-step mindset reset:

  1. Hit pause on DCA. Switch to reading mode. Thoroughly understand the principles.
  2. Rewire your thinking: when the index drops, you should feel happy — cheap shares are flooding in.
  3. If you truly cannot tolerate the drawdown, your understanding is not yet sufficient. Keep learning. Do not act emotionally.

9. The “Don’t Do” List

# Rule
1 Do not spend excessive time on investment research — when your capital is small, your time is best spent on career growth. That is where your fastest income gains will come from.
2 Never touch leverage, margin, or borrowed money for investing.
3 Never short the market.
4 Do not buy the top-10 performing funds of the past year (the champion’s curse — this year’s winner often becomes next year’s loser).
5 Never touch crude oil funds or commodity funds.
6 Do not go all-in — always maintain a margin of safety.
7 Do not act on emotion — don’t chase rallies, don’t panic-sell dips.
8 Do not abandon compounding — knowledge compounding and money compounding are the twin engines of financial freedom.

Any promise of high short-term returns carries extreme uncertainty. If you cannot bear the uncertainty of time, do not put money into a market that trades time for returns.
