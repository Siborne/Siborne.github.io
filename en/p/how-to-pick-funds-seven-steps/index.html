How to Pick Funds: The Seven-Step Framework

Picking funds is a learnable skill. This post breaks down the seven-step framework — from long-term holding and platform selection to fund company and manager evaluation — helping you build a systematic approach to fund investing.

The Core Insight: Funds Are Worth Holding for the Long Run

Top-tier funds can deliver 22%+ annualized returns over the long term. Yet nearly 40% of retail investors still lose money. The problem isn’t the funds themselves — it’s how you pick them and how long you hold them.

Funds have several structural advantages that are easy to overlook:

  • Even the weakest fund manager has an elite education and years of investment research experience — miles ahead of the average retail investor.
  • Every manager is backed by a professional research team with deep analytical capabilities.
  • If a manager underperforms, the fund company will replace them — a safety net that individual stock-pickers simply don’t have.
  • As long as you don’t lump-sum in at a bull-market peak, holding long-term means 95%+ of funds will eventually turn a profit.

Funds are not stocks. Do not trade them frequently. The tiered redemption fee structure exists precisely to incentivize long-term holding.

The Seven-Step Fund Selection Framework

Step 1: Ensure You Can Hold Long-Term

The first rule of fund investing is commit to holding long-term. The prerequisite: your invested capital must be idle money you won’t need anytime soon.

Avoid investing money that is:

  • Earmarked for a home purchase, tuition, or international travel within 1–2 months
  • Borrowed or leveraged
  • Your emergency fund

No matter how sound your investment philosophy may be, liquidity pressure will force you to sell at the worst possible moment.

Redemption fee tiers (for equity-heavy funds):

Holding Period Redemption Fee
< 7 days 1.50%
7 days ~ 1 year 0.50%
1 ~ 2 years 0.25%
> 2 years 0%

Zero fees after two years. This is the fund industry openly steering you toward long holding periods.

Step 2: Choose the Right Platform

Platform Type Pros Cons
Third-party platforms (Alipay, Tiantian, Qieman) Low fees, broad fund selection, fast settlement You need to do your own research
Commercial banks Personal service, simple process Extremely expensive (up to 10x third-party fees)
Direct from fund company Direct channel to the fund house Only that company’s products

Same fund, 1 million yuan invested: bank charges ~11,500 yuan in fees vs. ~1,000 yuan on Alipay — not even pocket change by comparison.

The verdict is clear: individual investors should default to third-party platforms. Fee differences compound over time — the money you save on fees is a meaningful source of returns in itself.

Step 3: Pick a Quality Fund Company

There is really one key metric: assets under management (AUM).

This isn’t an arbitrary number — it’s the result of investors voting with their feet:

  • Larger AUM → more management fee revenue → more resources to hire top managers and analysts → better performance → more inflows
  • It’s a virtuous flywheel

Public fund companies make their money primarily from management fees. Bigger firms have naturally deeper resources to invest in research infrastructure. Stick to major fund houses — your odds are simply better.

Step 4: Pick an Outstanding Fund Manager

Buying a fund is picking a manager. This is the single most important factor in your decision — bar none.

4.1 Years of Experience

The investment world is brutally unforgiving. Everything comes down to performance:

  • Incompetence or ethical issues → quickly eliminated by the market
  • Those who survive long-term do so because they’ve proven themselves
  • Longer tenure = richer experience = has lived through bull and bear cycles = better equipped for any market environment

An experienced driver may not be faster than a rookie, but they will definitely be steadier. In fund investing, steadiness matters far more than speed.

4.2 Track Record — Especially Drawdown Control

Don’t look at weekly or monthly returns. Don’t chase last year’s champions. Instead:

  • Zoom out: look for 3–5+ years of consistent profitability and disciplined drawdown control
  • A fund that doubles one year then halves the next is not an investment — it’s a rollercoaster gamble

The whole point of buying funds is stability. Whether you can hold on depends on whether the drawdowns exceed your comfort zone. Keep drawdowns within your tolerance, and you’ll be able to stay invested.

4.3 Job-Hopping Frequency

  • Strong performance + few job changes = high stability, a clear positive signal
  • Preference: managers who have spent most of their career at one firm → emotionally invested in the company → less tempted by distractions → consistently execute the firm’s investment philosophy

Many star public-fund managers are poached by private equity with massive pay packages. Those who “endure the quiet and guard their prime” are, by definition, acting in the best interests of their fund holders.

Step 5: Watch for the Right Fund Size

Size is the enemy of returns. This is an old saying in the investment world, and it holds true.

Size Range Potential Issues
Too large (tens or hundreds of billions) Hard to enter/exit positions, forced style drift, diluted alpha
Too small (sub-billion) Redemptions during downturns trigger forced selling → declining returns or even liquidation
Moderate (5–10 billion) ⭐ The sweet spot: flexibility plus economies of scale

Appropriate size is a bonus factor, not a dealbreaker. Zhang Kun delivered excellent returns at 100+ billion in AUM — though notably, he has since capped subscriptions, tacitly acknowledging that scale adds operational friction.

Step 6: Choose Diversified Funds

Spread your exposure across multiple themes and sectors.

Sector rotation is an enduring feature of the A-share market, and theme-chasing is hard to resist:

  • Single-theme funds: explosive returns when their sector is in favor; devastating drawdowns when it isn’t
  • Diversified funds: exposure across healthcare, consumer, tech, and more → far better drawdown control → a smoother ride for investors

Case in point: Zhang Kun (diversified across healthcare, consumer, and tech — steady drawdown profile) versus single-sector funds (all-in on semiconductors — wild volatility). Investors in the former almost certainly sleep better at night.

Step 7: Buy Old Funds, Not New Ones

Dimension New Fund Established Fund
Track record No history — marketing materials only Full historical performance, verifiable
Build-up period 3–6 months to deploy capital; will miss a bull run Fully deployed, in sync with the market
NAV Starts at 1, “looks cheap” May be much higher, “looks expensive”
Front-running risk New fund likely buys into existing funds’ top holdings → effectively front-running them No such issue

A fund’s investment value has absolutely nothing to do with its NAV! A NAV of 1 does not mean “cheap” and a NAV of 5 does not mean “expensive.” Funds make money from the underlying stocks appreciating, not from buying fund shares “low and selling high.”

Case study: E Fund Competitive Advantage attracted 230 billion yuan in subscription orders on a single day, against a cap of 15 billion — a 6% allocation rate. This tells you just how fundamentally most investors misunderstand how funds work.

Pro Tip: The Champion’s Curse

Never buy the top-10 funds by one-year return. This is a lesson the market has taught over and over.

In the history of China’s mutual fund industry, the previous year’s champion almost always suffers a massive drawdown the following year. Two reasons:

  1. Inflows dilute alpha: The championship year attracts a flood of capital, inflating AUM by orders of magnitude. The manager’s original strategy cannot scale to that level of capital.
  2. The champion was just in the right place at the right time: One explosive year does not prove a manager has genuine, repeatable alpha-generation ability. When the sector rotates away, the tide goes out — and you see who was swimming naked.

On Drawdowns and Volatility

  • Everyone calls you a genius when things are going up. Declines reveal who actually knows what they’re doing.
  • When evaluating a fund manager, drawdown control matters more than headline returns.
  • Extreme volatility → you will panic-sell → you will exit at the bottom → the subsequent recovery will not include you.

The ultimate goal of fund selection is not to find the fastest climber. It’s to find the one you can actually hold onto through the turbulence.
